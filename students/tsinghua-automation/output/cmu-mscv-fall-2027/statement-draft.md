# Statement Of Purpose Draft: Carnegie Mellon University - Master of Science in Computer Vision

**Review status:** Review Required

My undergraduate graduation project, Vision-Based Localization Methods for Mobile Robots, compared feature-based and direct visual odometry front ends on an indoor mobile robot across corridor, lobby, and laboratory sequences, and reported where each one lost tracking, how far the trajectory drifted before it did, and what happened when it tried to recover. I defended it in June 2026. It answered the question I had asked and immediately posed a harder one: I could say where localization degraded, but not how to correct it without making things worse. That is the question I have worked on since, as a research assistant on Loop-Closure Robustness for Visual SLAM in Repetitive Indoor Corridors. Corridors are a good adversary because their visual structure repeats, so appearance-based place recognition proposes closures that are geometrically impossible, and the pose graph accepts them. I am measuring how often that happens as a function of descriptor choice and geometric verification thresholds. The work is in progress and I am not yet willing to state a result. The pairing is what matters to me: the first project measured where localization drifts, and the second is measuring when the correction itself is wrong.

I have learned to build the measurement before the method. The Indoor Visual SLAM Evaluation Harness exists because I could not trust the numbers I had; it replays recorded robot sequences through several front ends and reports absolute trajectory error, relative pose error, and tracking-loss counts broken down per sequence, so a claim about one front end can be checked on the same data as any other. The Camera-IMU Calibration Toolkit for a Differential-Drive Robot came out of the same instinct after a hardware change quietly invalidated an earlier calibration and I had no way to detect it except by rerunning everything. Earlier still, in Uncertainty Calibration for Monocular Depth Estimation, I measured how badly a depth network's predicted confidence tracked its actual error on indoor scenes and reported calibration curves before and after temperature scaling. At the time I treated that as a side interest. It was the same question in another form: whether a model's confidence means anything is the question I now ask about whether a loop-closure score means anything.

The Robotics Institute lists, among the learning outcomes for the Master of Science in Computer Vision, applying mathematical and machine learning tools such as geometry, optimization, and statistics to computer vision applications, and conducting experimental analysis consistent with current practice, including standard metrics and benchmark datasets. That is a fair description of where my preparation is strong and where it stops. At Tsinghua University's Department of Automation I took Computer Vision (95/100), Pattern Recognition (93/100), Linear Algebra (92/100), and Probability Theory and Mathematical Statistics (91/100), and Optimal Control Theory (88/100) was my first sustained work on optimization as a subject rather than as a single algorithm. My overall result was 89.7/100 on Tsinghua's percentage scale, which I report as published rather than converted. The tools I work in daily, C++, Python, OpenCV, MATLAB, and LaTeX, are the ones the program's recommended skill set names. What I do not have is the depth these courses only gestured at: multi-view geometry past the undergraduate treatment, optimization on pose graphs rather than on textbook problems, and a real treatment of place recognition as a decision under uncertainty with an asymmetric cost.

That is why I am applying to MSCV in particular rather than to computer vision programs generally. The curriculum describes the core as addressing the two main areas of current computer vision systems, recognition and geometry, and places multi-view reconstruction and SLAM under the second; one of its core options pairs 16-822 Geometry-based Methods in Vision with 16-833 Robot Localization and Mapping. Those two courses are the syllabus for the gaps I just described, and I would take both if the schedule allowed. The shape of the program suits me as well as its content does. It is coursework-first over sixteen months with a hands-on team project as the culminating experience, and it requires a summer internship relevant to computer vision. Everything I have built so far has run on laboratory hardware and recorded sequences, chosen by me and evaluated against benchmarks I wrote. A two-semester team project and an industry internship are how I would find out which of those habits hold up when the system belongs to somebody else and the failure has a cost I did not define.

What I want from the next ten years is narrow and I would rather state it plainly: localization that stays reliable when a robot returns to a place it has already mapped and the place has changed, under different lighting, clutter, or season. The interesting version of that problem is perception for machines that work in the same building for years, not for one recorded sequence, and it is an engineering problem before it is a research one. Carnegie Mellon's MSCV is a professional degree pointed at exactly that kind of practice, which is what I am asking for. One practical note: my degree and transcript are issued in Chinese, and reconciling that official record with English application materials has been careful work in its own right. I will supply certified English documentation through whichever channel Carnegie Mellon specifies.
